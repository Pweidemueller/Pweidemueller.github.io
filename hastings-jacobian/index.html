Jacobians, Hastings ratios, and why your scale move needs them

A walk from the change-of-variables formula to the Metropolis–Hastings acceptance probability, ending with a live MCMC run that shows what happens when the Hastings correction is left out.

1 · Jacobians 2 · Jacobians in Hastings ratios 3 · Metropolis–Hastings 4 · Simulation

Jacobians: transforming probability distributions

A probability density is not a probability: it is probability per unit length (or area, volume). When you change variables, the units change, so the density has to be rescaled. That rescaling factor is the Jacobian.

Let \(X\) have density \(p_X(x)\), and let \(Y = g(X)\) with \(g\) invertible and differentiable. Probability mass in a small interval is conserved by the transformation:

\[ p_Y(y)\,|dy| \;=\; p_X(x)\,|dx| \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad p_Y(y) \;=\; p_X\!\big(g^{-1}(y)\big)\,\left|\frac{d\,g^{-1}(y)}{dy}\right| . \]

The factor \(|dx/dy|\) is the (1-D) Jacobian. Where \(g\) stretches space, the same mass is spread over a wider interval, so the density goes down; where \(g\) compresses space, the density goes up. In several dimensions, \(\mathbf{y}=g(\mathbf{x})\), the factor becomes the absolute determinant of the Jacobian matrix:

\[ p_{\mathbf Y}(\mathbf y) = p_{\mathbf X}\big(g^{-1}(\mathbf y)\big)\,\left|\det \frac{\partial g^{-1}(\mathbf y)}{\partial \mathbf y}\right| . \]

Two examples we'll reuse

(a) Log-normal multiplier. Draw \(k\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\) and set \(m=e^{k}\). Then \(k=\log m\), \(dk/dm = 1/m\), and

\[ p_M(m) = \phi_\sigma(\log m)\cdot\frac{1}{m}, \]

which is the log-normal density. The \(1/m\) is the Jacobian. Forgetting it gives the wrong shape (the curve labelled "no Jacobian" below), which doesn't even integrate to 1.

(b) An exponential prior seen on the log scale. If \(X\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)\) and \(Y=\log X\), then \(x=e^y\), \(dx/dy=e^y\), and

\[ p_Y(y) = \lambda e^{-\lambda e^{y}}\cdot e^{y}. \]

This one matters later: a sampler that moves in \(\log x\) is effectively sampling \(Y\), and has to target this density, Jacobian included.

Where you meet this in practice: reparameterising a model (sampling \(\log\) rates instead of rates), putting a prior on a transformed quantity, normalising flows, and, as the next section shows, any MCMC proposal built by drawing a random number and pushing it through a deterministic function.

Why Jacobians appear in Hastings ratios

Metropolis–Hastings (section 3) accepts a proposed move \(x\to x'\) with probability

\[ \alpha(x\to x') = \min\!\left\{1,\; \frac{\pi(x')}{\pi(x)}\cdot \underbrace{\frac{q(x\mid x')}{q(x'\mid x)}}_{\text{Hastings ratio}}\right\}. \]

The Hastings ratio corrects for asymmetry in the proposal: if the proposal reaches \(x'\) from \(x\) more easily than it reaches \(x\) from \(x'\), the chain would over-visit \(x'\) unless that bias is cancelled. The correction comes from detailed balance, the condition that at equilibrium the flow of probability \(x\to x'\) equals the flow \(x'\to x\):

\[ \pi(x)\,q(x'\mid x)\,\alpha(x\to x') \;=\; \pi(x')\,q(x\mid x')\,\alpha(x'\to x). \]

The Jacobian enters through \(q\)

Many moves are not written as a density \(q(x'\mid x)\) directly. Instead you draw a random variable and apply a deterministic map. The scale (multiplier) move used in MrBayes, RevBayes, BEAST and others is:

\[ k\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2),\qquad x' = x\cdot e^{k}. \]

The density of \(k\) is symmetric, but we need the density of \(x'\), and getting it is a change of variables (section 1): \(k=\log x' - \log x\), \(\;dk/dx' = 1/x'\), so

\[ q(x'\mid x) = \phi_\sigma(\log x'-\log x)\cdot\frac{1}{x'},\qquad q(x\mid x') = \phi_\sigma(\log x-\log x')\cdot\frac{1}{x}. \]

The normal parts are equal (\(\phi\) is symmetric) and cancel, leaving only the Jacobians:

\[ \boxed{\;\frac{q(x\mid x')}{q(x'\mid x)} = \frac{x'}{x} = e^{k}\;} \]

Green's formulation (reversible jump). The same answer comes from treating the move as a deterministic map of the pair \((x,k)\) to \((x',k')\) with \(k'=-k\) (the draw that would take you back). Then

\[ \alpha=\min\!\left\{1,\;\frac{\pi(x')\,g(k')}{\pi(x)\,g(k)}\left|\frac{\partial(x',k')}{\partial(x,k)}\right|\right\},\qquad \left|\det\begin{pmatrix} e^{k} & x e^{k}\\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix}\right| = e^{k}. \]

With \(g(k')=g(-k)=g(k)\), the "Hastings ratio" is literally the Jacobian determinant of the move. That's why people say "the Hastings ratio of a scale move is the scaler".

What the correction means

  • Geometric view. The move is symmetric in \(\log x\), not in \(x\). From a large \(x\), a given spread in \(k\) covers a wide interval of \(x\)-values, so each specific \(x'\) is less likely to be proposed. Expanding moves (\(k>0\)) therefore get a bonus \(e^{k}>1\), and contracting moves get a penalty \(e^{k}<1\).
  • Target-transform view. Equivalently, the chain is really a symmetric random walk on \(y=\log x\). A symmetric random walk on \(y\) needs the target density in \(y\), which (example b above) is \(\pi(e^y)\,e^{y}\). The extra \(e^{y'}/e^{y} = x'/x\) is the same factor. Leave it out and you're sampling \(p_Y(y)\propto\pi(e^{y})\), which in \(x\) is \(\propto \pi(x)/x\): a different, and here improper, distribution.

Metropolis–Hastings, piece by piece

The general algorithm

Goal: draw samples from a target density \(f(x)\) that we can evaluate only up to a constant, \(f(x)=\tilde f(x)/Z\).

  1. Start at some \(x^{(0)}\).
  2. Propose \(x'\sim q(\cdot\mid x)\).
  3. Compute the acceptance probability \[ \alpha = \min\!\left\{1,\;\frac{\tilde f(x')}{\tilde f(x)}\cdot\frac{q(x\mid x')}{q(x'\mid x)}\right\}. \] The unknown constant \(Z\) cancels, which is the whole reason MH is useful.
  4. Draw \(u\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)\). Accept if \(u<\alpha\): set \(x^{(t+1)}=x'\). Otherwise reject: set \(x^{(t+1)}=x\).
  5. Record \(x^{(t+1)}\) and repeat.

A rejection still produces a sample. The current value is recorded again. Dropping rejected steps (recording only accepted states) biases the chain.

ElementMeaning
\(f(x)\) / \(\pi(x)\)Target (stationary) distribution; needed only up to a constant.
\(q(x'\mid x)\)Proposal distribution: how candidate moves are generated.
\(\tilde f(x')/\tilde f(x)\)Target ratio: always accept uphill; accept downhill sometimes.
\(q(x\mid x')/q(x'\mid x)\)Hastings ratio: corrects proposal asymmetry (=1 for symmetric proposals, giving plain Metropolis). Contains the Jacobian when the move is a transformation.
\(\alpha\)Acceptance probability, capped at 1.
\(u\)Uniform random number; accept iff \(u<\alpha\) (in practice \(\log u<\log\alpha\)).

In Bayesian MCMC

The target is the posterior:

\[ f(\theta) = p(\theta\mid D) = \frac{p(D\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)}{p(D)} \;\propto\; \underbrace{p(D\mid\theta)}_{\text{likelihood}}\;\underbrace{p(\theta)}_{\text{prior}} . \]

The marginal likelihood \(p(D)\) is the intractable constant, and it cancels. The acceptance ratio factors into three familiar pieces:

\[ R \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{p(D\mid\theta')}{p(D\mid\theta)}}_{\text{likelihood ratio}}\;\times\;\underbrace{\frac{p(\theta')}{p(\theta)}}_{\text{prior ratio}}\;\times\;\underbrace{\frac{q(\theta\mid\theta')}{q(\theta'\mid\theta)}}_{\text{Hastings ratio (incl. Jacobian)}},\qquad \alpha=\min(1,R). \]

Software works on the log scale: \(\log R = \Delta\log L + \Delta\log\text{prior} + \log\text{HR}\), and accepts if \(\log u < \log R\).

No data ⇒ posterior = prior. If there's no data (or the likelihood is a constant, e.g. "run under the prior" in MrBayes/BEAST/RevBayes), the likelihood ratio is 1 and the correct sampler must return the prior. This is a standard test for whether a move's Hastings ratio is right, and it's exactly what the simulation below does.

Simulation: a multiplier move with and without the correction

Setup: prior \(X\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)\), no data, so the target is \(\pi(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\). Each step draws \(k\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\), proposes \(x'=x\,e^{k}\), and computes

\[ \alpha_{\text{Hastings}} = \min\!\Big\{1,\; e^{-\lambda(x'-x)}\cdot e^{k}\Big\},\qquad \alpha_{\text{no Hastings}} = \min\!\Big\{1,\; e^{-\lambda(x'-x)}\Big\}. \]

Two chains run side by side and use the same random numbers (\(k\) and \(u\)) at every step, so the only difference between them is the \(e^{k}\) term. Changing a slider restarts the run.

What you should see

  • With Hastings (green): the histogram converges to the \(\text{Exp}(\lambda)\) prior and the sample mean to \(1/\lambda\). Smaller σ means higher acceptance but slower exploration; very large σ means many rejections. Both reach the same answer; only efficiency differs.
  • Without Hastings (orange): the chain drifts toward 0 and piles up there. It's doing a correct Metropolis walk on \(\log x\) with target \(\propto\pi(x)\), which in \(x\) means density \(\propto e^{-\lambda x}/x\). That function isn't integrable at 0, so there's no proper stationary distribution: once \(x\ll 1/\lambda\) the prior ratio is ≈1, every move is accepted, and \(\log x\) wanders freely toward \(-\infty\). Switch on the log y-axis to watch it.

So, is it true that a correct Hastings ratio makes the chain sample the prior? Yes. With no data the posterior is the prior, so with \(\text{HR}=x'/x\) the stationary distribution is exactly \(\text{Exp}(\lambda)\), and the histogram of \(X\) approaches the prior as the run gets longer (in distribution, after burn-in, with Monte Carlo noise shrinking like \(1/\sqrt{\text{ESS}}\)). Without the correction the chain is not merely "a bit off": here it targets an improper distribution and collapses toward 0.

Implementation note: the chains are stored as \(\log x\) so the uncorrected chain can wander to extremely small values without underflowing the arithmetic. Both chains share the same \(k\) and \(u\) at each step (common random numbers).
